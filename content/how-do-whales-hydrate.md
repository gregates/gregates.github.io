A wonder of the modern world is that we all have tiny computers in our pockets that can quickly
retrieve vast quantities of information. Because of this, it's common to assume that if something is
knowable, it can be known simply by pulling out this computer and typing in the right question. A
decade ago, the place one typed this question was a search engine called "Google". I was in graduate
school, in philosophy, to observe the transition from "I wonder if X? Let's wildly speculate or make
an educated guess about X!" (this one was a favorite passtime of philosophy grad students) to "I
wonder if X? Let's google it!".

It is trivial to observe that not everything that is knowable is known. Even if we accept
the premise that everything that is known is encoded as information retrievable from our pocket
computers, it follows that not everything that is knowable is googleable. Therefore, the common
assumption is false.

In fact, the common assumption is pernicious precisely because it entails that everything that is
knowable is known. This is obviously false if you
think about it even for a second. And yet, it's common practice to assume that everything knowable,
or at least everything worth knowing, or at least everything *you* might want to know, is known.

If one does think about it for a second, one might admit that there are things that are knowable but
unknown, yet still assume that there is a sort of "frontier" of scientific knowledge that has
expanded greatly in our own lifetimes, such that now only esoteric, cutting-edge stuff outside this
frontier remains unknown &mdash; quantum physics, what's going on in far-flung corners of universe,
extremely difficult mathematics that you've never heard of and never will, stuff like that. But the
observable world around us is basically well-understood at this point, right? The sort of questions
most people would think up and wonder about &mdash; we can just ask google. Some smart scientist has
already figured it out and written down the answer.

Because of this, it's better to illustrate with a counterexample. My favorite is: "How do whales
hydrate?" The answer to this question is knowable. And it's worth knowing every bit as much as any
other fact of marine biology. It's not some trivial thing that I can claim we don't know through
some trick of epistemology or logic &mdash; it's just a question about the natural world we happen
not to know the answer to.

Every time I have asked someone this question, they have immediately assumed that the answer is both
known and googleable. I do not believe it is known, but I'm not certain of that &mdash; perhaps
there is some marine biologist out there who has answered this question, and I just haven't learned
about it yet. I can't say for sure. But I can show you that it's not googleable.

## Google results

When I first developed this counterexample, as I remember it, the google results made it relatively clear that the
science on whale hydration was unsettled (though some results overstated the strength of the
evidence for various hypotheses). This is less true today. For example, at the time of
writing, the first page of google results includes a short blog post from a Dr. Richard Johnson,
apparently a professor of medicine at the University of Colorado. The entirety of the blog post
reads:

> You may have never thought about this question, but how do whales get water?  Ocean water turns out to be too salty for the whale, so the whale gets most of its water from the food it eats, such as from fish, krill and plankton. However, a lot of the water the what gets is from its fat, for when fat is broken down, it produces water—which is sometimes referred to as metabolic water.  One of the reasons the whale is the fattest animal in the world is that it has substantial water needs.  The observation that fat serves as a water source was instrumental in the discovery that dehydration is a stimulus to gain fat.

This post then references two academic journal articles, giving it a patina of scholarly authority.
But a perusal of those sources reveals no direct evidence for the claims in the post beyond
establishing some plausibility as hypotheses. That is not the same as no evidence, but an untested
hypothesis is not knowledge.

Other sources acknowledge the limitations of our knowledge to some extent, but nevertheless provide
definitive-sounding answers to the question. For example, the website
[whalescientists.com](https://whalescientists.com/do-whales-drink/) addresses this question by
confidently asserting

> Whales (whether they are toothed whales or baleen whales) do not voluntarily ingest water. At least not that we really know of. They find another way to “make” their own water. How? By transforming their food into the water.

despite going on to conclude that "The question remains unanswered today" several paragraphs later.

This [*Scientific
American* article](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-can-sea-mammals-drink/) does, in my
opinion, a better job of discussing the state of our knowledge and some plausible hypotheses without
overstating the case for any one hypothesis, and concludes with a clear statement that

> For most whales and dolphins, however, we simply do not know how they get their water, because it is difficult to observe these animals.

So anyone who reads through the top results and considers them carefully should come away with the
conclusion that we don't know how whales hydrate. Unfortunately, if you only look at the first
result (as of this writing), you will find a reddit post where someone asked this question. The answers make it clear
that other people also googled this question, read the same sources I discuss above, and took away
a different lesson. One redditor, for example, quotes the very *Scientific American*
article I do above, but instead of quoting the sentence that says we don't know, instead chooses to
quote only this:

> sea-dwelling mammals can get water through their food, and they can produce it internally from the metabolic breakdown of food

In other words, they quote one plausible hypothesis, and leave it at that.

This shows why the assumption that everything knowable is googleable is pernicious. Because googling
can easily lead you to assume that something is known when it is not.

## LLM results

Of course, today one may be more likely to ask a chat bot backed by a large language model (LLM)
rather than google. So I recently decided to try that out, to see if it's any better. My LLM of
choice is Claude.

(**Disclaimer:** in general, I think it is a sound principle never to reproduce and ask people to
read the output of your own chat sessions with an LLM. In this case, I will make an exception,
because the point of this post is to discuss the quality of that output, rather than to suggest that
it as worth reading.)

As LLMs are wont to do, Claude initially answered my question by enumerating *every* hypothesis that
you might find by googling and asserting that the explanation is the combination of these factors.
I've truncated the output for brevity, because LLMs are so, so verbose.

> Whales have evolved several clever adaptations to stay hydrated while living in saltwater:

> **Metabolic water production:** …

> **Efficient kidneys:** …

> **Minimal water loss:** …

> **Food moisture:** …

> Interestingly, while whales inevitably ingest some seawater while feeding, their bodies are efficient enough to handle and excrete the excess salt without becoming dehydrated. It's a beautifully balanced system that allows these massive mammals to thrive in an environment that would quickly dehydrate most other animals!

No hint whatsoever that this is still an open question. And, unlike google, where there are many
results you can read and weigh against one another to try to get a more complete picture, the LLM
just asserts.

Of course, you can ask follow-up questions. So I asked it for sources, and it gave me some for each
of its four hypotheses above, using its own ability to perform a google search. These sources were,
unsurprisingly, the same ones I noted above (plus something from the Dutch Shark Society on the
question of reduced water loss).

Perhaps interestingly, Claude here actually gave me sources for **five** claims, including the claim
that whales routinely drink seawater, which it notably had not asserted in the previous answer.
Nevertheless, it offered a correction on this one point (citing the *Scientific American* article
and whalescientists.com).

> Whether whales routinely drink seawater: I should correct this - it's actually not well
> established that whales routinely drink seawater voluntarily, though some may ingest it
> incidentally while feeding.

Claude at this point concluded:

> So my initial explanation was generally accurate, though I should have been clearer that most whales probably don't voluntarily drink seawater regularly - they mainly rely on metabolic water from their food!

So I asked it directly what evidence there is for that conclusion, other than mere plausibility as a
hypothesis. At this point it finally produced an answer that included these sentences:

> You've hit on a crucial point. The honest answer from the scientific literature is: we don't actually know for certain.

It then cited the same sentences from our sources that I excerpted above, which state that the
answer to this question remains unknown. So the right prompting got it to focus on the relevant
sentences from its sources,
unlike the reddit answer google gave us. But the straightforward question instead produced just
confident bluster (that was at least reflective of the going hypotheses).

## Conclusion

We don't know how whales hydrate, but it's easy to mistakenly conclude that we do by relying on your
pocket computer. Be skeptical.
